Exploring 1,174 Exoplanets

A scroll through what one snapshot of the exoplanet catalogue actually says — how these worlds were found, what they're like, which (if any) could be habitable, and the record-holders. Every chart is hand-drawn from the raw CSV with no libraries, and every chart tells you how much data it had to leave out.

How to read this page. Each panel has a one-line plain-language takeaway and a small grey caption reporting N used / N missing — empty cells in the data are counted, never silently dropped. Dotted terms like equilibrium temperature have hover definitions. Colour always means the same discovery method across every chart (see legend).

Selection bias is real

This sample is what our instruments can detect, not what exists. Transit and radial-velocity surveys favour large, close-in planets ("hot Jupiters"); imaging favours massive planets on wide orbits. Small, cool, distant worlds are systematically under-counted — their apparent rarity here is partly a property of our telescopes.

Some temperatures are estimated

When pl_eqt_computed=True, the equilibrium temperature was computed from the star and orbit, not measured. The habitability box flags these in amber, because a candidacy that hinges on an estimate is weaker than one that doesn't.

Stars vs planets

Multi-planet systems repeat their host star across rows, so a star is keyed by star_id, not its name. Planet-level counts use rows; system-level records (like biggest system) group by star_id.
